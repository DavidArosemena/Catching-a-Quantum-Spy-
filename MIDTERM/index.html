Beginning · Question & context

A qubit is a point on a sphere

What is a qubit, and how is it different from a classical bit that can only be 0 or 1?

A classical bit lives at one of two places: the north pole |0⟩ or the south pole |1⟩. A qubit can point anywhere on the surface of this sphere, the Bloch sphere.

Two angles describe it. θ (theta) is the tilt away from |0⟩, and φ (phi) is the turn around the equator:

|ψ⟩ = cos(θ/2)|0⟩ + eiφ sin(θ/2)|1⟩
Try this: drag the white dot on the arrow, or use the sliders. Drag empty space to orbit the sphere (double click to reset the camera). Snap to the named states and notice that |+⟩ and |−⟩ sit on the equator, exactly halfway between 0 and 1.

Why 3D: the set of all qubit states really is a sphere. A flat chart would hide φ, which matters later for gates.

Beginning · Question & context

Measuring turns an arrow into odds

If a qubit can point “in between,” what do we actually see when we measure it?

Measuring always gives a plain 0 or 1, and the qubit collapses to that pole. The arrow only sets the odds:

P(0) = cos²(θ/2)    P(1) = sin²(θ/2)

One measurement tells you almost nothing. Only after many fresh copies do the bars settle onto the dashed predictions.

Key idea for later: switch the measurement basis to X. A qubit on a pole (|0⟩) now gives a coin flip, 50/50. Measuring in the “wrong” direction scrambles the answer. BB84 is built on this.

Middle · Mechanism

Gates rotate the qubit, and the basis matters

How do we prepare qubits in two different directions, and why does reading in the wrong one scramble the answer?

A quantum gate is a rotation of the arrow. Each gate here is a half turn (180°) around the dashed axis:

  • H (Hadamard): axis halfway between x and z. Swaps |0⟩ ↔ |+⟩ and |1⟩ ↔ |−⟩.
  • X (bit flip): axis x. Swaps |0⟩ ↔ |1⟩.
  • Z (phase flip): axis z. Swaps |+⟩ ↔ |−⟩.
Try this: run H on |0⟩. You get |+⟩, which reads 100% certain in X but a 50/50 coin flip in Z. Alice will use exactly these two “directions” (bases) to hide her key.

Middle · Mechanism

The BB84 key exchange

How can two people build a shared secret key out of random qubit measurements?

  1. Alice picks a random bit and a random basis (Z or X) and sends that qubit.
  2. Bob picks his own random basis and measures.
  3. They publicly compare bases only, never bits. Where the bases match, Bob's bit must equal Alice's. They keep those (the sifted key) and discard the rest.
What to notice: about half the qubits are discarded, and with nobody listening, the kept bits match perfectly: 0 errors. That is our baseline.

Middle · Exploration

Enter Eve: intercept and resend

What physically happens when Eve measures a qubit in transit, and why can't she just copy it?

Eve cannot copy an unknown qubit (the no cloning theorem). Her only option is to measure it in a guessed basis and send Bob a fresh qubit matching what she saw.

Half the time she guesses the wrong basis. Her measurement is then a coin flip, she resends the wrong state, and Bob's bit is wrong half of those times, even when Bob and Alice agree on the basis.

Ending · Evidence

The error rate reveals the spy

Across many runs, how does the quantum bit error rate (QBER) grow with Eve's interception, and can honest noise be confused with a spy?

Class question: if Eve intercepts half of the qubits, what error rate will Alice and Bob see in their sifted key?

Each dot pools many simulated runs (total errors ÷ total sifted bits) with a 95% error bar. Lines are the theory:

QBER = e + p − 2ep,   e = f/4
What to notice: errors rise in a straight line with f, up to about 25%. Now raise the channel noise p: the whole curve lifts. Alice and Bob can't tell a noise error from a spy error, so noise gives Eve cover and eats into the safety margin below the red threshold.

07
Ending · Insight & limitations

How many bits until we're sure?

How many sifted bits must Alice and Bob sacrifice to be confident Eve is there, and what does this model leave out?

Alice and Bob reveal m random sifted bits and compare them. Each one catches Eve with probability f/4, so she survives all m checks with probability (1 − f/4)m:

P(detect) = 1 − (1 − f/4)m

What this model leaves out

  • Perfect single photons. Real lasers sometimes emit two, which opens photon number splitting attacks.
  • No photon loss and perfect detectors. Real links lose most photons over distance.
  • Eve only uses intercept and resend. Smarter collective attacks leak less noise.
  • The detection rule here assumes no channel noise (p = 0). With noise, Alice and Bob compare the QBER to a threshold instead (View 6).
  • The public channel must be authenticated, or Eve simply pretends to be Bob.

So, can Eve stay hidden?

1

Measuring a qubit in the wrong basis gives a random answer, and it changes the qubit. Eve cannot avoid that.

2

Intercept and resend pushes the sifted error rate toward 25%, growing in a straight line with how much she taps (f/4).

3

Checking a few dozen bits exposes a full interception with more than 99% confidence, but noise can give a careful spy some cover.

Quantum key distribution protects the key. The broader defense against quantum attacks on today's encryption is post quantum cryptography (NIST FIPS 203).

Behind the story

Model, data, and verification

Everything above comes from one documented simulation. Here is the model, the data it produces, and the checks that confirm it.

1 · Model and assumptions

|ψ⟩ = cos(θ/2)|0⟩ + eiφsin(θ/2)|1⟩  →  r = (sinθ cosφ, sinθ sinφ, cosθ)
P(0) = (1 + rz)/2 = cos²(θ/2)    P(+) = (1 + rx)/2
QBER = e + p − 2ep, e = f/4    P(detect) = 1 − (1 − f/4)m

Per qubit, the simulator makes exactly 8 seeded random draws (Alice bit, Alice basis, Eve on?, Eve basis, Eve coin, Bob basis, Bob coin, noise flip). Assumptions: ideal single photons, no loss, intercept and resend only, independent bit flip noise p, authenticated public channel. Random numbers come from the seeded mulberry32 generator, so every run is reproducible.

SELECT scenario, f_pct, p_pct,
       SUM(sifted) AS sifted_bits, SUM(error) AS error_bits,
       ROUND(100.0 * SUM(error) / NULLIF(SUM(sifted), 0), 2) AS qber_pct   -- pooled, not averaged
FROM qubit_events GROUP BY scenario, f_pct, p_pct;

Why pooled: runs keep different numbers of sifted bits, so averaging per run percentages would overweight short runs. NULLIF turns an empty sifted key into “n/a” instead of a divide by zero.
